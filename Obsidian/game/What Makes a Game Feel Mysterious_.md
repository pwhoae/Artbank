### The Answer

**21:39** · And so after all of that - we finally get the actual answer.

**21:44** · Perhaps it's a handy upgrade, or some bonus content, maybe an alternative ending, or a satisfying revelation.

**21:52** · But to be honest: if the act of solving the mystery is really fun, then the answer doesn't particularly matter.

**21:59** · I absolutely loved figuring out the cryptic game-wide scavenger hunt to open Tunic's mysterious mountain door.

**22:06** · But I literally can't remember what was behind it.

**22:10** · So the answer doesn't matter too much - unless it gives away the answer to the game's other questions.

**22:19** · Here's what I mean by that.

**22:20** · Going back to Elden Ring - the first time you come across, say, a catacomb, it's a potent source of mystery.

**22:28** · What is this place? What will I find inside? Well, the only way to know is to ready your sword and shield and dive on in.

**22:36** · But then later, you find another catacomb.

**22:40** · And then another.

**22:42** · And another still.

**22:44** · And each one seems to cohere to a strict template: there are white flowers, there's a boss, and your reward for beating the dungeon is almost always a new spirit ash.

**22:56** · Soon enough, the player will start to notice the pattern, and can now predict the answer to a question before they've even had a chance to answer it.

**23:05** · Which essentially robs the question of its mystery.

**23:09** · So while it's tempting to use a repeating template to design content - especially if you're making a massive game that requires huge amounts of stuff for players to discover - it's important to make sure players can't predict the answers to everything.

**23:23** · So this can be achieved by making sure you also litter in a few completely unique areas that don't follow any kind of pattern.

**23:32** · Or by establishing a pattern...

**23:34** · and then intentionally breaking it.

**23:37** · Like how in Super Metroid, the game establishes that item rooms are safe places to get a new ability or upgrade - but then you get the bomb from this room, and...

**23:48** · oh right - I wasn't expecting that! Now you'll know not to take things for granted.

### Invisible Questions

**23:55** · So - that's it, right? A game can pose questions - and the player can discover answers.

**24:02** · Well - this neat and tidy categorisation does miss one thing.

**24:07** · Because for the player to ask a question, they need to know that something is being concealed in the first place.

**24:14** · You can only wonder what's behind a door...

**24:17** · if you know that the door exists.

**24:19** · But what if you never actually show the door to the player?

**24:23** · Well, let's talk about The Witness.

**24:26** · And skip ahead to the timestamp on screen if you don't want to be spoiled on this one.

**24:31** · So this is a game about solving back-of-a-cereal-box grid puzzles on a beautiful Myst-like island.

**24:38** · And those puzzles are pretty neat.

**24:41** · Each batch of head-scratchers is based on some special rule - perhaps shadows or symmetry or sound or separation - and you'll need to figure out the underlying language of the puzzle design in order to solve all of the grids.

**24:56** · But - there's more to The Witness than meets the eye.

**24:59** · Because after hours and hours of staring at grids and lines, you'll perhaps start to notice something.

**25:07** · Like, maybe you've climbed all the way to the top of this mountain, you look down at the river below and you think...

**25:14** · wait, doesn't that look like one of the lines I've been drawing on these grids?

**25:18** · And so you press the button - the one that, so far, you've only ever used to draw on a grid panel.

**25:25** · Only this time, you're drawing on the world itself.

**25:28** · And - holy crap! There are puzzles in the environment! Forget those poxy grid panels - every path, every flower, every cloud could now be a puzzle.

**25:40** · This mind-blowing moment of revelation changes everything.

**25:44** · It turns out that there are questions that we didn't even know we could answer.

**25:48** · Invisible questions.

**25:50** · Ones that make you ask "what else is the game hiding?"

**25:54** · Tunic developer Andrew Shouldice says "my very favourite sort of game is one where as you play, you don't just find new things in your path, but suddenly the entire path that you've traveled becomes littered with question marks."

**26:07** · And it's not just The Witness.

**26:09** · The first time you hit a wall in Dark Souls and it disappears in a puff of smoke - well, your entire understanding of the world just changed.

**26:17** · What else could be hidden behind one of these illusory walls? Chests? Goodies? Surely not an entire extra area...

**26:26** · Same goes for realising that you can input codes into Fez by jamming on the shoulder buttons.

**26:32** · Or that you can escape into the user interface in Super Mario Bros., and run straight past the level goal.

**26:38** · Uncovering one of these mysteries feels like discovering a true secret about the world around you.

**26:44** · And for that to be the case, these invisible questions pretty much have to be entirely optional.

**26:51** · Something that only certain players will stumble upon.

**26:53** · The Witness designer Jonathan Blow says "it had to be legitimately likely that you could finish the game and never see that stuff.

**27:01** · Like you had to take an extra step of noticing something on your own to find that."

### Conclusion

**27:07** · So: we can make a game mysterious by concealing something from the player.

**27:11** · It could be a gigantic locked door.

**27:14** · It could be the rules and mechanics of the game itself.

**27:17** · It could be the landmarks and layout of a fantasy world.

**27:21** · Or it could be an enigma at the heart of the game's narrative.

**27:25** · Each one of these things creates a question in the players mind.

**27:29** · And then by letting the player be the one to actually answer this question - through discovery, exploration, observation, puzzle-solving, or even working with others, the player gets the incredible satisfaction of being the one to crack the code, and reveal the secret.

**27:47** · But if we don't even let on that there's a puzzle to solve - if we just let it sit there, in the player's view, subtly hinting that there's more than meets the eye - we can give the player a true moment of mystery, as they realise that there's more to this game than they ever thought possible.
